Can your phone tell what your body is doing?

Joshua Do · November 2026

Introduction

The ExtraSensory dataset was collected at UC San Diego in 2015 and 2016. Sixty volunteers carried their own phones through their normal days, and most of them also wore a smartwatch. About once a minute, an app recorded 20 seconds of sensor data and saved it as 225 summary features covering motion, rotation, compass direction, sound, location, light and phone state. The volunteers also reported what they were doing, using 51 labels. The dataset has 377,346 rows, one per person per minute.

We want to know whether a phone's motion sensors can tell what someone's body is doing (lying down, sitting, standing, walking, running or bicycling), including for people the model has never seen.

Phones have the same motion sensors robots use to track their own movement: an accelerometer, a gyroscope and a magnetometer. A robot that works next to people, like an exoskeleton or a care robot, needs to know whether the person is up and moving or lying down. On a phone, the same kind of model could power habit tracking.

The columns we use:

  • label:LYING_DOWN, label:SITTING, label:OR_standing, label:FIX_walking, label:FIX_running, label:BICYCLING: the body state the person reported for that minute (1 = yes, 0 = no, blank = not reported).
  • raw_acc:magnitude_stats:std: phone shaking, or how much the phone's acceleration varied during the recording, in G.
  • watch_acceleration:magnitude_stats:std: the same measure for the watch, in milli-G.
  • raw_acc:3d:mean_z: the phone's average acceleration along the axis that points out of the screen, in G. When the phone is still, this is gravity.
  • timestamp: when the minute was recorded, in Unix seconds (UTC).
  • uuid: an anonymous ID for each user.
  • Phone type (iPhone or Android), which comes from the dataset's cv_5_folds files.

Data Cleaning and Exploratory Data Analysis

  • We combined the six body-state labels into one column, body_state. No row has two body states. A blank label means the person didn't answer, so blanks stay missing instead of turning into 0.
  • We replaced impossible readings with missing values: 2,186 barometer readings of exactly 0 hPa, all from one user, and two negative autocorrelation periods in a single row.
  • We dropped 4 columns that have the same value in every row.
  • We converted timestamps to San Diego time and added hour and weekday columns.
  • We added each user's phone type. 34 users had iPhones and 26 had Androids.
  • iPhones and Androids point the accelerometer's axes in opposite directions, so we flipped the sign of the iPhone readings in raw_acc:3d:mean_x, raw_acc:3d:mean_y and raw_acc:3d:mean_z to match Android.
  • We dropped the other 45 labels and label_source. People reported these at the same moment as their body state, so a model wouldn't have them when it makes a prediction.
  • We kept the 306,594 minutes that have a body state, which still covers all 60 users. Missing sensor values stay missing.

Assessment of Missingness

We think the body-state labels are NMAR. 18.7% of minutes have no body state, and whether someone stops to answer probably depends on what they're doing. Someone in the middle of a workout or driving is less likely to answer than someone sitting at a desk. App logs showing which reminders people ignored could make this missingness MAR.

Watch shaking is missing in 31.5% of minutes. We ran two permutation tests on its missingness, with total variation distance (TVD) as the test statistic and a significance level of 0.05.

  • Missingness appears to depend on body_state. The observed TVD is 0.0997, the largest of 1,000 shuffled TVDs is 0.0074, and the p-value is below 0.001.
  • We found no evidence that it depends on the minute of the hour. The observed TVD is 0.0063 and the p-value is 1.000.

Hypothesis Testing

Does the phone type change what the sensors report when people are doing the same thing? This matters because the final model will use phone orientation, and the fairness analysis compares iPhone and Android users.

We look at the z-axis of the accelerometer (raw_acc:3d:mean_z), which points out of the screen. When a phone is still, this reading is gravity: about +1 G when the phone lies screen-up and −1 G when it lies screen-down. We only use sitting minutes, so both groups are doing the same thing, and for each of the 60 users we compute the share of their sitting minutes with a positive z reading.

  • Null hypothesis: among sitting minutes, a user's share of positive z readings has the same distribution for iPhone users and Android users, and any difference is due to random chance in which users have which phone.
  • Alternative hypothesis: the share of positive z readings is different for iPhone users and Android users.
  • Test statistic: the absolute difference between the two groups' mean share. We had no reason to expect either phone type to be higher, so the test has no direction.
  • Significance level: 0.05.

Phone type belongs to a person, not to a minute, so we use one number per user and shuffle the phone types across the 60 users 10,000 times. Shuffling minutes would treat one person's thousands of minutes as independent.

Each dot is one user. The average iPhone user has a positive z reading in 83.9% of their sitting minutes, and the average Android user in 82.0%.

The observed difference is 0.019. About 64% of the shuffled differences are at least that large, so the p-value is 0.639, well above 0.05, and we fail to reject the null hypothesis. The data are consistent with iPhone and Android users holding their phones the same way while sitting. Repeating the test within each of the six body states gives the same answer, with p-values between 0.20 and 0.91.

Framing a Prediction Problem

We predict each minute's body state from that minute's sensor readings. This is multiclass classification with six classes, and the response variable is body_state.

We score models with macro F1, the average of the six per-class F1 scores. Sitting and lying down are 78% of minutes, so a model could get high accuracy while ignoring the rare states. Macro F1 counts every state equally.

When a recording ends, we know its sensor readings, the hour and the phone type. We don't know any of the labels the person reported, so none of them are used as features.

We split by user, with 48 users (251,590 minutes) for training and 12 users (55,004 minutes) for testing. Every test score comes from people the model never saw.

Baseline Model

The baseline is a decision tree with a maximum depth of 8, inside one sklearn Pipeline. It uses two features:

  • raw_acc:magnitude_stats:std (quantitative): phone shaking. We fill missing values with the training median.
  • hour (nominal, 0 to 23): the hour of the day, one-hot encoded.

On the 12 test users, the baseline gets a macro F1 of 0.387 and 67.4% accuracy. Always guessing "sitting" gets a macro F1 of 0.106. The training macro F1 is 0.386, almost the same as on the test users.

For the final model, we plan to add the watch accelerometer, gyroscope, audio, location speed and phone-state features. Phone orientation (raw_acc:3d:mean_x/y/z) might help separate sitting, standing and lying down. We'll also add a column that marks when the watch is missing, since that depends on body state, and each user's motion over the previous few minutes. For the model itself, we'll try a random forest or gradient boosting, tuned with GridSearchCV and GroupKFold by user and scored by macro F1.
